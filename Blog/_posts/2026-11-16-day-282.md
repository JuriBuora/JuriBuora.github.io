# 🔄 Topic

A calendar write and its owner notification can have different outcomes. I reviewed the persistent notification queue added during the Ubuntu migration to keep an unsuccessful send from disappearing behind a successful action.

# 🎯 Goal

Preserve pending notices across runs, retry them even when no new input arrives, and make the limits of delivery evidence explicit.

# 🛠 What I Reviewed

The implementation persists the pending notice alongside the local action state. Later runs retry the queue independently of new incoming messages. A backoff prevents every run from immediately repeating a failed attempt.

The transport adapter accepts success only when the Telegram response has the expected success value, a valid message identifier, and the configured destination. A vague truthy result is insufficient. The saved receipt contains acceptance time and message identifier rather than a copy of the transport response.

The queue also binds each notice to its intended target. If that target is missing or differs from the current configuration, the pending notice is held instead of being redirected automatically.

# 🔍 Why the Bounds Matter

The inspected implementation caps pending notices at 50 and expires notices older than 48 hours. A full queue pauses further calendar writes so new actions do not silently accumulate beyond the notice capacity. Expired notices are counted and flagged.

The input watermark—the position up to which incoming work is considered processed—also needs careful handling. A failed calendar write initially holds that position for retry. This policy is bounded: after three write failures, the implementation gives up on that window and logs the decision. Queue saturation has its own hold behavior.

That distinction matters. I cannot describe the system as retrying every failed action forever, or as guaranteeing that no work can ever be dropped. Its limits are explicit operational decisions that need visibility.

# 🧪 What the Tests Covered

The inspected fixtures use a fake notifier, fake calendar helper, and fixed clock. They cover an outage followed by a retry without new messages, early retries held by backoff, target mismatch, queue saturation, expiry, and overlapping runs refused by a lock.

One crash case interrupts a send after the action state has been saved. The next run retries the notice with a possible-duplicate warning. A successful ordinary retry does not repeat the calendar action in that fixture.

# 🔗 Key Cybersecurity Connections

This connects availability, destination integrity, and auditability. A pending notice must retain its destination boundary during recovery, and an operator needs evidence that distinguishes an action from notification acceptance.

There is also an unavoidable uncertainty in the implemented protocol: the remote service may accept a message just before the process crashes without saving the local receipt. Retrying can then duplicate the notice. The design records that uncertainty rather than claiming exactly-once delivery.

# ⚠ Evidence Limits

The migration record includes acceptance by the real Telegram API. That is separate from the synthetic outage tests and does not prove a person read the message or a device displayed it. This article does not add any live send.

# 📚 What I Learned

Persistence needs a policy for retry, expiry, capacity, destination changes, and uncertain sends. A queue without those decisions can preserve work while hiding a growing failure.

# 😄 TL;DR

I reviewed a persistent, bounded notice queue that retries independently of new input, checks the destination, and exposes expiry and duplicate uncertainty. API acceptance remains distinct from human receipt.
